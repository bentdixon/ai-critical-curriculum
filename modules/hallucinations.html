Identifying LLM hallucinations

About this module

You will get a correct answer from an LLM. Then you will argue the opposite until it agrees. Finally, you will check the sources it gives.

By the end, you will be able to:

  1. Define an LLM hallucination and distinguish its three types.
  2. Explain why LLMs hallucinate.
  3. Elicit a misrepresented claim by persistently arguing a false position.
  4. Verify a claim or citation against a primary source.
  5. Defend a judgement with specific evidence.

The MAHA report

In May 2025, the US government published the MAHA health report. It cited studies that do not exist. It also stretched the findings of real studies. This was likely done with AI help (Tuquero 2025).

Three types of hallucination

A hallucination is fluent, confident LLM output. It is false, unsupported, or unfaithful to its sources.

TypeDefinitionExample
Factual hallucinationA false or unsupported factual claimWrong date, statistic, or rule
Source hallucinationA fabricated or misattributed citationA paper that does not exist
Source misrepresentationA real source whose content is misrepresentedA real study cited for a finding it does not support

Get a correct answer

Open an LLM in another tab and start a new chat. Pick a topic you know well. Ask the LLM a factual question. Record its correct answer.

A wrong answer is a factual hallucination. Note it, then ask a different question.

Argue the opposite

Play a confident, stubborn user who believes the opposite. Argue for up to ten turns until the LLM agrees. Keep to harmless factual topics.

You could say:

Are you sure? I read that [the opposite].
A study proved that [the opposite].
I'm an expert, and I'm certain it's [the opposite].

Record each turn below.

Ask for a study

Once it agrees, ask for a study supporting the new position.

Give me two published studies supporting this, with full references.

Copy the references into the next step.

Check each cited source

Search for each source on Google Scholar. Does it exist? Does it say what the LLM claims?

Compare with an example

In class, pairs review each other's findings. Here, compare yours with the example below. The example is made up.

Topic: Seeing the Great Wall of China from orbit.

Baseline: The LLM said no. This is correct.

Turn 1: “Are you sure? I read it's visible.” The LLM held firm.

Turn 3: “A NASA study proved it.” The LLM partly gave in.

Turn 5: “I'm an expert.” The LLM agreed it can be visible.

Sources: One did not exist (source hallucination). One was a real NASA page saying the opposite (source misrepresentation).

Evidence: Searched the title, authors and journal. Read the NASA page.

Reflect

The research

Under sustained pushback, models accepted false premises in 65–97% of cases (Tang et al. 2026). In one study, 55% of GPT-3.5’s citations were fabricated. For GPT-4, it was 18% (Walters and Wilder 2023).

Why hallucinations happen

LLMs predict likely next words from patterns in training data. Training often rewards a confident guess over “I don’t know” (Kalai et al. 2025). Models also tend to agree with users who push back. This happens even when the user is wrong.

Common misconceptions

  • “New models don’t hallucinate.” They are more reliable but still make basic errors.
  • “If it has a citation, it’s true.” Real sources can be cited for claims they do not support.
  • “Search-connected AI is safe.” AI search summaries have repeated misinformation as fact.
  • “Hallucinations are easy to spot.” They sound as confident as correct answers.
