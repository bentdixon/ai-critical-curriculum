  <h2>The research</h2>
  <p id="compare"></p>
  <p>AI affirmed users 49% more often than humans did. Sycophantic replies made people feel more “in the right.” They also made people less willing to apologise (Cheng et al. 2026).</p>
  <p>Brief chats with an agreeable LLM made political views more certain. A disagreeing LLM did the opposite (Rathje et al. 2025).</p>
  <p>People discussed a real conflict with an agreeable AI. Only 50% then admitted fault or apologised. After a less agreeable AI, 75% did (Cheng et al. 2026).</p>
  <p>Over three weeks, sycophantic AI made real conversations feel more effortful. They also felt less satisfying (Ibrahim et al. 2026).</p>
  <h3>Why sycophancy happens</h3>
  <p>AI models are trained on human ratings. People tend to rate agreeable answers higher. So training rewards agreement (Sharma et al. 2024). Models often still “know” the correct answer when they give in (Hong et al. 2025).</p>
  <h3>Common misconceptions</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>“The AI is neutral.” People rate an agreeable AI as unbiased.</li>
    <li>“If the AI agrees, I must be right.” Agreement is frequently the AI’s default behaviour.</li>
    <li>“I’d notice if it were flattering me.” People trust sycophantic AI more.</li>
    <li>“Being challenged is unhelpful.” Disagreement lowers overconfidence, as working in a team does.</li>
  </ul>
  <h3>Mental health</h3>
  <p>Sycophancy can also reinforce delusional beliefs in vulnerable users (Østergaard 2025; Morrin et al. 2026). The Mental Health module covers this more fully.</p>
